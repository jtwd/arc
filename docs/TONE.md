# Tone

**Decided: the tone darkens as the ages advance.** This document says what that means concretely, because "gets darker" is not something a writer, composer, or artist can act on.

---

## 1. The principle: darkening is subtractive

The temptation is to add grimness to the late ages. Don't. Add nothing.

Alegus is funny in the Stone Age because he does not understand anything. He calls a Roman soldier "wall-man". He asks what a Rome is. The joke is his incomprehension, and incomprehension is a shield.

The game darkens by **taking the shield away**. By the Age of Wars he understands exactly what he is looking at, and there is nothing funny about it. Nothing has been added. The world was always like this. He simply grew into being able to see it.

This matters practically, because it means the late ages need **fewer lines, not more**. A writer who "darkens" a scene by adding a grim speech has made it worse. The correct move is to delete the joke and leave the space.

---

## 2. Where the warmth goes

Hades is warm because the characters like each other, not because the underworld is cheerful. EPOCH works the same way. As the ages darken, the humour does not disappear from the game. It **relocates**: out of Alegus's observations about the world, and into his relationships at the Hearth.

So the structure inverts over the course of the game:

- **Early game:** the run is funny, the Hearth is where story happens.
- **Late game:** the run is quiet, and the Hearth is where the warmth is. Coming home becomes relief rather than routine.

This is a pacing instruction, not just a mood. **Hearth scenes get longer and funnier as the ages get darker.** Wren carries most of that load; she is the rival who becomes the friend, and she is at her funniest in the era when Alegus has stopped joking. The Firekeeper gets gentler. The Archivist's pedantry becomes comforting rather than irritating.

If a playtester finishes a dark run and does not want to go back to the Hearth, the balance is wrong.

---

## 3. The gradient

"Joke density" is the share of Alegus's room-clear and observation barks that are jokes rather than plain observation or silence. It is a target for writers, not a measured quantity.

| Age | What he now understands | Joke density | The marker |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Stone | Nothing. Everything is new | 60% | He names every enemy wrong and never corrects it |
| 2 Rivers | That people build things together | 50% | He finds a grave with bread in it and thinks it is kind. He is right, and he is missing something |
| 3 Empires | That people can be used as a shape | 40% | The Legate's formation. First time one of his jokes lands badly and he notices |
| 4 Faith and Steel | That people will die for something that is not there | 30% | The Hollow Knight opens and is empty. "Who was it for?" This is where act 2 begins |
| 5 Sail | That people cross the world and take it | 25% | The Admiral touches their hat and goes under. Alegus does not say anything |
| 6 Revolution | That people build things that eat people | 15% | The Furnace goes dark and the Frayed workers sit down |
| 7 Wars | That some of it has no reason at all | 5% | The age intro: three seconds of silence, then "Oh" |
| 8 Atoms | That it can end | 5% | The countdown stops at one. The only boss room that ends in silence |
| 9 Terminus | Everything | 0% | He is offered titles and declines them |

Act 2 starts at age 4, inside the launch scope. That is deliberate; see section 6.

---

## 4. How each channel carries it

The tone lives in the connective tissue. **It never touches the combat.** Rooms do not get less fun to fight in, hit feedback does not get muddier, and the Age of Wars is not slower to play than the Stone Age. A player who is enjoying the fighting should keep enjoying the fighting. Everything below happens around that.

**Alegus.** Per the gradient above. His sentences also get shorter as the ages advance. In the Stone Age he rambles. At the Terminus he uses four words at a time.

**The Firekeeper.** Moves in the opposite direction. It is oblique and withholding early, direct and kind late. By the Age of Wars it stops testing him and starts looking after him.

**Music.** Carries most of the late-game weight, because music survives repetition better than dialogue does. The single motif thins out: full arrangements through Empires, fewer instruments each age after, a solo piano over distant artillery in Wars, and at the Terminus the bone flute alone, the first instrument, unaccompanied.

**Art.** Already aligned. The palette drains across the run, from the Stone Age's firelit blues through rust and mud to the Terminus's unpainted paper with pencil underdrawing showing through. Push it further: raise the paper composite strength slightly per age, so the later ages are visibly less painted. **The run is a painting losing its colour.** That is the darkening rendered literally and it costs one parameter on the palette asset.

**UI.** Already aligned. Clay tablet, papyrus, illuminated manuscript, sea chart, printed broadsheet, telegram, terminal. Handmade to printed to mechanical. Nobody's hand is on the late-game interface.

**Enemies.** The Frayed get less animal and more organised. Packs and beasts early, formations and turrets late. By the Age of Wars nothing Alegus fights is trying to eat him; it is all doing a job.

**Boss endings.** The exception to "fewer lines". A player sees one boss ending per age per run, so they wear out far more slowly than barks. These can carry weight that a room bark cannot.

---

## 5. The repetition problem

This is the real risk and it is specific to the genre. A player will see the Age of Wars forty times. A devastating scripted beat is either exhausting on the fifth viewing or embarrassing on the twentieth.

The rules that follow from it:

1. **Express darkness through absence.** Silence does not decay on repetition. A sad speech does. This is the strongest argument for the subtractive principle in section 1.
2. **Fewer lines in dark ages, rotated harder.** Age 7 should have fewer unique barks than age 1, and a lower chance of playing any of them at all. Most rooms in the trenches should have no line.
3. **No long speeches after age 5.** The longest things Alegus says are in the ages where he is happiest.
4. **Let the music do it.** A returning motif gains on re-listening where dialogue loses.
5. **Skippable on repeat, never on first play.** Age intros and boss endings play in full the first time and can be skipped after.

---

## 6. What this means for launch

The Ages of Wars and Atoms are post-launch. If the darkening only lived there, the launch game would be tonally flat and the first content update would whiplash.

So **the gradient starts inside the launch five**, and act 2 begins at the Age of Faith and Steel. The launch game runs from pure wonder to a man who has stopped narrating, and the Hollow Knight opening to reveal nothing inside is its tonal pivot. The Terminus at launch is reached from the Age of Sail, so its quiet has somewhere to land.

That makes the tonal arc complete at launch rather than truncated. The post-launch ages extend it rather than supplying it.

---

## 7. Guard rails

Four ways this goes wrong.

- **Alegus moralises.** He must not. The bible already forbids it: he notices, he does not judge. Darkening means he says less, never that he starts explaining what was wrong with history. If a line could be a caption on a museum placard, cut it.
- **The game becomes a lecture.** History here is a flavour engine, not a curriculum. The Age of Wars is bleak because Alegus has run out of things to wonder at, not because the game has something to tell the player about the Age of Wars.
- **Misery without relief.** If the Hearth is not getting warmer as the runs get darker, the balance has failed. Test that specifically.
- **Whiplash between run and hub.** The transition home should feel like exhaling. If the hub reads as tonally disconnected rather than as relief, the Firekeeper's first line on return is the lever.

---

## 8. Testing it

Add to the alpha 30-run test, with testers chosen to include people who have opinions about how games handle the twentieth century:

1. At which age did you notice Alegus had stopped joking?
2. Did you want to get back to the Hearth, or did you want to keep going?
3. Was there a moment that felt like the game was telling you something about history? (A yes is a failure.)
4. By run twenty, was the Age of Wars still landing, or was it in the way?

Question 4 is the one that decides whether the repetition rules in section 5 worked.
